# San Francisco leaves rules, data, and climate unjoined

San Francisco has plenty of housing data and plenty of human help, but **no public tool answers housing questions with citations that combine the governing law, the city's housing data, and climate-risk layers**, and that is true for policy makers and residents alike. The official code host, American Legal Publishing, offers only keyword search ([Univ. at Buffalo guide](https://research.lib.buffalo.edu/american-legal-pub-code-library)). The city's housing dashboards and the DataSF portal have no official natural-language query layer ([SF Planning Housing Dashboard](https://sfplanning.org/node/3312)). Its sea-level-rise and flood layers are open but old ([data.gov: 108" zone](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/108-inundation-vulnerability-zone-line-sea-level-rise-100yr-flood-event)), and nobody overlays them on housing sites. Inside City Hall, AI use is real but generic. About 30,000 employees got Microsoft Copilot Chat in July 2025 ([sf.gov](https://www.sf.gov/news-mayor-lurie-brings-ai-technology-to-san-francisco-government-cementing-city-as-global-ai-leader)). The one serious statutory-AI project, Stanford RegLab's STARA with the City Attorney, targeted reporting requirements, not housing ([Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/policy/cleaning-up-policy-sludge-an-ai-statutory-research-system)). None of the housing departments (Planning, DBI, MOHCD, the Rent Board) appears in the city's own AI inventory ([DataSF 22J API](https://data.sf.gov/resource/9tj4-z32q.json)). Tenants depend on phone lines, clinics, and right-to-counsel lawyers, and no SF agency or nonprofit runs a tenant chatbot. City rules add a constraint for any tool aimed at government: SF's generative-AI guidelines prohibit "relying on AI to review legal or regulatory issues" ([sf.gov guidelines](https://www.sf.gov/san-francisco-generative-ai-guidelines)). So the opening is real, and it has to be framed as cited research support, not legal determination.

The pattern is clear. Parcel and zoning data is crowded and commoditized. Official housing data is plentiful. AI over the law is either internal (STARA), lawyer-priced (CoCounsel, Lexis+ AI), or unofficial and unevaluated (Justee, custom GPTs). Climate appears only in standalone hazard tools.

### Code search stops at keywords, while the code keeps moving

SF's codes are free to read on American Legal Publishing. Searches run on 2026-11-03 found **no AmLegal AI search feature**, and the publisher's staff cannot interpret the law ([Univ. at Buffalo guide](https://research.lib.buffalo.edu/american-legal-pub-code-library)). The rival hosts use ElasticSearch with Boolean operators ([CivicPlus help](https://www.civicplus.help/codification/docs/advanced-search-options)) or multicode keyword search ([General Code](https://generalcode.com/online-code/)), and neither hosts SF anyway. The rules people most need to read, the Rent Ordinance (Admin. Code Ch. 37) and the Planning Code, have no chatbot. The only AI-related item found in Chapter 37 is §37.10C, which bans algorithmic rent-setting devices, with penalties up to $1,000 per violation ([SF Rent Board](https://www.sf.gov/information/algorithmic-devices-set-rent-are-prohibited-san-francisco)). The code is also a moving target. SF Planning's SB 79 implementation touches **about 120,000 parcels in 270 transit-stop areas**, and low-resource parcels are temporarily exempt until January 2032 ([SF Planning](https://sfplanning.org/project/senate-bill-79-implementation-san-francisco)). The Family Zoning Plan must accommodate at least 36,200 more units toward an 82,000-unit Housing Element target, and it faces two lawsuits ([SF Planning FZP PDF](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/citywide/fzp-sb-79-compliance.pdf); [Coblentz Law](https://www.coblentzlaw.com/?p=12251)). Vendors that encode zoning as data will lag fast-changing overlays like these. The researcher's inference is that the differentiator is reasoning, with citations, about *which rules apply and why*.

### Data supply is abundant; interpretation is not

The official data layer is deep. The Planning Housing Dashboard (launched January 2025) maps completed units and the active pipeline by neighborhood and supervisor district ([SF Planning](https://sfplanning.org/node/3312)). The Q2 2026 pipeline report lists **75,853 pipeline units, 18,536 of them affordable (24%)**, and Planning itself warns of errors from mixed sources ([SF Planning pipeline report](https://sfplanning.org/project/pipeline-report)). The Rent Board Housing Inventory covers about 250,000 rent-ordinance units and refreshes every 24 hours, but the data is self-reported and not verified ([data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/rent-board-housing-inventory)). Statewide, HCD's APR dashboard tracks RHNA progress, again from self-reported data ([HCD](https://hcd.ca.gov/housing-open-data-tools/apr-dashboard)). Natural-language access exists only through third parties: a 2023 "San Francisco GPT" prototype *(possibly outdated)* ([Axios SF](https://www.axios.com/local/san-francisco/2023/04/19/san-francisco-gpt-data-portal)) and generic DataSF connectors ([Glama](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/pr2xxaqtt1); [Runbear](https://runbear.io/use-cases-software/san-francisco-open-data-datasf)). These return raw query results with no policy context. Legislative tracking has the same gap. Plural's AI bill summaries focus on states, and SF depth is unverified ([Plural](https://pluralpolicy.com/ai-powered-bill-tracking/)). Soar publishes summaries of Board meetings, but they are not linked to code sections ([Soar](https://cpo.soar.com/articles/7875758/San-Francisco-County/California/Supervisors-approve-amendment-allowing-planning-director-to-update-SB-79-exemption-tables-pending-HCD-determinations)). Research tools such as the Terner Center dashboard and the Urban Displacement map are mostly static and several years old *(possibly outdated)* ([Planetizen](https://planetizen.com/node/86670/housing-development-dashboard-promises-new-understanding-housing-policy)).

## City Hall runs on Copilot, and housing departments report no AI

### Generic AI is everywhere; housing-specific AI is absent from the record

SF's confirmed staff AI deployment is **Microsoft 365 Copilot Chat for nearly 30,000 employees**, launched July 14, 2025. It runs on GPT-4o in Microsoft's Government Community Cloud at no added license cost ([sf.gov press release](https://www.sf.gov/news-mayor-lurie-brings-ai-technology-to-san-francisco-government-cementing-city-as-global-ai-leader)). It followed a ChatGPT pilot with more than 2,000 staff. In that pilot, 70% of 554 surveyed participants reported saving up to 5 hours a week, and the Department of Technology funded 250 ChatGPT Enterprise licenses *(June 2025; possibly outdated)* ([Civil Grand Jury report](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/2025_CGJ_Report_AI_Techs_in_the_City.pdf)). The Chronicle reports that adoption is uneven. Usage hovered around half of employees between July 2025 and April 2026, highest among lawyers, political staff, and administrators *(search summary only; paywalled)* ([SF Chronicle](https://www.sfchronicle.com/sf/article/ai-city-worker-san-francisco-22326139.php)).

The city's AI inventory, required by Admin. Code Chapter 22J, held **52 entries from 11 department codes** when the researchers pulled it on 2026-11-03. **None came from Planning, DBI, MOHCD, the Rent Board, the Board of Supervisors, the Controller, or the Department of the Environment** ([DataSF 22J API](https://data.sf.gov/resource/9tj4-z32q.json)). The only housing-adjacent entries are OCII's Copilot use for meeting notes and HSH's Polimorphic RV-ordinance assistant (same source). The empty rows could mean no use, or they could mean non-compliance. The Voice of SF found only 8 of 50+ departments had reported, that the CIO "has no authority to compel reporting", and that internal-administration tools like Copilot appear to be exempt ([The Voice of SF](https://thevoicesf.org/san-franciscos-secret-ai/)). PermitSF launched February 13, 2026 on a contested $5.9M OpenGov contract. No source documents AI plan review in it ([SF Standard](https://sfstandard.com/2026/05/13/daniel-lurie-opengov-permitting-permitsf-san-francisco/); [Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2026/06/mayor-luries-permitting-contract-report/)). The Civil Grand Jury called COIT's claim of AI at the Permit Center "overstated", since the tool there is an SMS queue (QLess) *(June 2025; possibly outdated)* ([CGJ report](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/2025_CGJ_Report_AI_Techs_in_the_City.pdf)). Private vendors market AI pre-checks of DBI plans, but these are not city programs ([InspectMind](https://www.inspectmind.ai/plan-check-in/san-francisco-ca-permit-review)). The state now offers Archistar's AI plan check free to any local government ([StateScoop](https://statescoop.com/?p=70712)).

### STARA is the one statutory-AI precedent, and it was politically charged

The most concrete use of AI in SF legislative work is **STARA**. The City Attorney's Office has partnered with Stanford RegLab since 2024. STARA scanned about 16 million words of Municipal Code and about 16,000 Board resolutions, chunked each provision with its definitions and cross-references, and produced a cited, searchable database. Attorneys verified every output by hand ([Reboot Democracy](https://rebootdemocracy.ai/blog/sf-ai-regulatory-decluttering)). It found **528 reporting requirements**. The resulting ordinance proposed removing 140, consolidating 34, and keeping 314 (same source). That split likely reconciles the conflicting press counts of 174 ([Daily Journal](https://dailyjournal.com/articles/385997)) and 140 ([SF Examiner](https://www.sfexaminer.com/news/the-city/sf-city-attorney-turns-to-ai-to-cut-down-on-policy-sludge/article_56f86a1c-84df-4610-b50d-e2a7accb7667.html)): 140 + 34 = 174. This reconciliation is an inference, not stated by the sources. The 362-page ordinance passed only **7-4 on July 14, 2026**. The Clerk called the single-vote approach a "deviation", and **Supervisor Myrna Melgar, who voted no, said she used Anthropic's Claude to understand the changes** ([Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2026/07/sf-city-code-streamlining-mandelman/)). Melgar's use was personal, not a City-provisioned tool. No AI use by the Budget and Legislative Analyst was found ([sf.gov Board of Supervisors](https://www.sf.gov/departments--board-supervisors)). STARA's lesson cuts both ways. SF's legal staff accept AI for code-wide statutory search when every output is cited and checked by hand, but bulk AI-assisted changes draw scrutiny when they reach a vote.

### The rules make legal-review AI a high-risk category

The July 2025 Generative AI Guidelines matter most for positioning. Using AI to "summarize policy-related data" is medium to high risk and requires expert review, disclosure, and a 22J inventory entry. **"Relying on AI to review legal or regulatory issues" is prohibited** ([sf.gov guidelines](https://www.sf.gov/san-francisco-generative-ai-guidelines); [July 2025 guidelines page](https://www.sf.gov/reports--july-2025--san-francisco-generative-ai-guidelines)). Each tool has a ceiling on data sensitivity: Level 4 for Copilot and Snowflake, Level 3 for ChatGPT Enterprise. Consumer tools need departmental approval (same sources). The sf.gov page shows "Last updated: September 30, 2026", but what changed could not be confirmed *(unverified)*. COIT promised an AI Strategy, an AI Policy, and a beta citywide "AI Workspace" with pre-approved models before the end of 2026. It also described a "Try Before You Buy" procurement framework for limited AI pilots ([COIT ICT Plan Update](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/COIT_2026_ICT_Plan_Update_April_2026.pdf)). None of these could be confirmed as launched *(unverified)*. No SF statement prefers open-source or locally hosted AI. The city's stated direction is cloud-first ("Cloud 2030") (same source).

## Climate data exists in silos, with no housing overlay

### Flood and sea-level layers are open but dated, and each covers something different

SF's sea-level-rise layers are on DataSF under a public-domain PDDL license. The **108" Inundation Vulnerability Zone** (dataset `92e4-7ptg`) combines extreme SLR with a king tide and a 100-year storm. It is a "planning level tool" built on **2010 ground conditions** *(possibly outdated)* ([data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/108-inundation-vulnerability-zone-line-sea-level-rise-100yr-flood-event)). It comes from SF Planning's 2020 Sea Level Rise Vulnerability and Consequences Assessment *(possibly outdated)* ([SLRVCA report](https://default.sfplanning.org/plans-and-programs/planning-for-the-city/sea-level-rise/SLRVCA_Report_00.pdf)). The SFPUC 100-Year Storm Flood Risk Zone covers storm runoff only, not Bay or ocean inundation. SFPUC updates it each year by July 1, and the DataSF copy is from July 2022 *(possibly outdated)* ([data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/100-year-storm-flood-risk-zone-july-2022); [SFPUC](https://sfpuc.org/learning/emergency-preparedness/flood-maps)). BCDC's ART Flood Explorer models 0" to 108" of SLR for the Bay shoreline only, not SF's Pacific coast, and no license statement was found for its downloads *(search summary only)* ([ABAG](https://abag.ca.gov/technical-assistance/art-bay-shoreline-flood-explorer)). The researcher's key inference: **any answer about whether a parcel is in a flood zone must name the map it used**, because these three layers measure different hazards. FEMA flood maps and Our Coast Our Future were not researched.

### Heat, equity, and seismic layers, and the decarbonization rules

CalEnviroScreen 5.0 became final on July 1, 2026, the first update since 2021, along with a preliminary Disadvantaged Communities designation ([OEHHA](https://oehha.ca.gov/calenviroscreen/press-release/press-release-calenviroscreen/california-releases-calenviroscreen-50-identify-and-prioritize-pollution-burdened-communities)). Cal-Adapt's Analytics Engine serves downscaled CMIP6 data at about 3 km, and its `climakitae` toolkit is BSD-3 licensed ([AWS Open Data](https://registry.opendata.aws/caladapt-coproduced-climate-data/); [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/climakitae)). SFDPH's Heat Vulnerability Index covers 574 block groups with 21 variables, but it predates 2017 *(possibly outdated)* ([Adaptation Clearinghouse](https://www.adaptationclearinghouse.org/resources/climate-and-health-understanding-the-risk-an-assessment-of-san-francisco-eys-vulnerability-to-extreme-heat-events.html)). DBI's weekly Soft-Story Properties dataset links seismic risk to wood-frame buildings with 5+ units. The 4,802-building count and the 98% compliance figure are old *(possibly outdated)* ([ESIP Soft Story](https://sfgov.org/esip/soft-story)).

For decarbonization, SF has required **all-electric new construction since June 1, 2021** ([Earthjustice](https://earthjustice.org/?p=9443)). The **All-Electric Major Renovations Ordinance** passed unanimously on 2025-09-02 and took effect in July 2026 ([SFEnvironment](https://www.sfenvironment.org/press/board-supervisors-passes-major-renovations-ordinance-setting-course-all-electric-future)). Its legal text is Board File 250702, a PDF ([File No. 250702](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/File_No._250702_All-Electric.pdf)). An industry source says it was codified at §304.5 of the 2025 SF Existing Building Code *(unverified)* ([BOMA SF](https://bomasf.org/resources/building-codes-regulatory-compliance)). The critique that it raises housing costs is on record ([Reason](https://reason.com/2025/08/12/san-franciscos-all-electric-ordinance-will-make-housing-more-expensive/)). A 2021 BLA memo put residential gas-appliance conversion at roughly $3.2B–$5.9B citywide *(2021; range from a partial snippet, verify)* ([BLA memo](https://sfbos.org/sites/default/files/BLA.ResidentialDecarbonization.042221.pdf)). No structured, machine-readable version of these code texts was found.

### Policy makers have no tool that joins housing sites to climate risk

The 2022–2031 Housing Element's rezoning program **excludes sites in areas susceptible to SLR** *(search summary only)* ([Appendix B](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/citywide/housing-choice/housingchoice_element_appendixB_sites_inventory.pdf)). Its EIR was certified on November 17, 2022 ([CEQAnet](https://ceqanet.lci.ca.gov/Project/2021060358)). Yet **no public SF tool overlays Housing Element sites or the housing pipeline on flood, SLR, heat, or CalEnviroScreen layers**. No direct evidence was found that the Board, the Planning Commission, or MOHCD uses any combined housing-climate tool. Absence from public search does not prove that no internal tool exists. The equity side is thin too. The SF Climate Equity Hub is a retrofit-rebate program, not a data tool. It received $370,878 in FY2024-25, and the next budget was reported to provide none ([SF Public Press](https://www.sfpublicpress.org/mayors-recommended-budget-sparks-debate-over-s-f-climate-priorities/)). The Urban Displacement Project studies displacement driven by climate *investment*, not SLR exposure ([SGC](https://sgc.ca.gov/grant-programs/ccr/findings/investment_related_displacement/)). No SF agency licenses First Street, whose customers are investors and banks and which MSCI agreed to buy for $120M on 2026-06-24 ([ESG Today](https://www.esgtoday.com/msci-acquires-climate-risk-data-provider-first-street/)). The researcher inferred, without verifying, that the main climate exposure in new housing sits in shoreline development agreements such as Mission Rock, Pier 70, and Treasure Island, rather than in rezoned sites. The Port's roughly $13.5B Waterfront Coastal Flood Study plan still awaited congressional authorization ([Port StoryMap](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/1ed3561c936244f2979ad68cda6a681a)), and its status as of November 2026 is unconfirmed.

## Tenants get people, not tools, and AI help is all pilots elsewhere

SF's official tenant resources are informational. The Rent Board offers topic pages in English and four other languages, phone and in-person counseling (415-252-4600), and a property portal. **It has no interactive coverage checker, no separate calculator, and no chatbot** ([SF Rent Board](https://sf.gov/departments/rent-board)). The allowable increase for March 2026 to February 2027 is **1.6%** ([sf.gov](https://www.sf.gov/news--annual-rent-increase-3126-22827-announced)). DAHLIA handles affordable-housing lotteries, but eligibility is set building by building, so "am I eligible?" has no general answer ([sf.gov](https://sf.gov/information/creating-online-affordable-housing-portal)). Nonprofit help is human. HRCSF counsels more than 5,000 tenants a year ([HRCSF](https://www.hrcsf.org/who-we-are)). SFTU runs drop-in clinics ([SFTU](https://sftu.org/)). EDC leads Tenant Right to Counsel, which SF voters created in 2018 as the first such program in any US city ([Next City](https://nextcity.org/urbanist-news/a-win-for-tenants-rights-in-historic-prop-f-vote-in-san-francisco)). The TRC collaborative reported a **93% success rate** in the eviction cases it handled ([BayLegal](https://baylegal.org/tenant-right-to-counsel-prevents-homelessness/)). Capacity is the constraint. Reporting from 2024 found too few experienced tenant attorneys *(possibly outdated)* ([SF Standard](https://sfstandard.com/2024/01/09/california-evictions-tenants-right-to-counsel-san-francisco/)). Tenants Together's hotline call-backs have taken weeks *(possibly outdated)* ([Tenants Together](https://www.tenantstogether.org/es/node/3291)). Demand is rising with AI-boom rents ([SF Standard](https://sfstandard.com/2026/08/03/ai-boom-san-francisco-economy-renters-rent-spike/); [Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2025/09/sf-evictions-rents-rising-ai-boom/)). The hardest question a tenant faces, whether their unit falls under the SF Rent Ordinance, AB 1482 only, or neither, depends on facts such as building age and ownership type. AB 1482, for example, exempts buildings less than 15 years old ([Berkeley Rent Board](https://rentboard.berkeleyca.gov/laws-regulations/state-law/ab-1482-california-tenant-protection-act-2019)). No official tool asks those questions.

**No SF government agency or SF nonprofit runs a tenant AI tool.** California's AI legal aid is at the pilot stage and aimed at courts and legal-aid staff. Contra Costa Superior Court has a beta chatbot, "Coco" ([Contra Costa court](https://contracosta.courts.ca.gov/self-help/landlord-tenant)). Stanford is testing AI error checks for unrepresented tenants with LA Superior Court *(search summary only; article returned 403)* ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/08/ai-tools-tenant-cases-los-angeles-superior-court)). Stanford's Legal Design Lab is building Gates-funded eviction-defense co-pilots with LA legal aid groups ([Stanford LDL](https://justiceinnovation.law.stanford.edu/justice-ai-co-pilots)). Three warning cases bound the design. The FTC's final order against DoNotPay's "robot lawyer" required $193,000 in relief and bars claims of equaling a human lawyer without evidence ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/87474)). Closer to home, SF's own Polimorphic RV chatbot gave wrong eligibility answers and wrong dates, and the vendor said the city was responsible for checking responses ([Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2025/10/s-f-launched-an-ai-chatbot-for-rv-dwellers-its-got-errors/)). Stanford found that the most harmful AI errors on tenant questions are **procedural** (wrong deadlines, wrong courts, wrong steps), and that too many disclaimers can stop people from acting ([Stanford LDL rubric](https://justiceinnovation.law.stanford.edu/measuring-what-matters-a-quality-rubric-for-legal-ai-answers)).

## Gaps and positioning: the opening is the join, with citations

Five gaps recur across all four research streams. First, **no public AI tool answers questions over SF's housing law with citations.** STARA shows it can be done, but it is internal and was not built for housing ([Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/policy/cleaning-up-policy-sludge-an-ai-statutory-research-system)). Second, **no tool links housing data to the law that gives it meaning.** RHNA progress, pipeline counts, and rent-ordinance coverage sit on dashboards with no rule context, and generic DataSF connectors return only raw rows ([Glama](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/pr2xxaqtt1)). Third, **no tool overlays housing sites, the pipeline, or rent-controlled stock on climate layers**, even though every input is open: the PDDL-licensed DataSF flood and SLR layers, CalEnviroScreen 5.0, and the soft-story feed ([data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/108-inundation-vulnerability-zone-line-sea-level-rise-100yr-flood-event); [OEHHA](https://oehha.ca.gov/calenviroscreen/press-release/press-release-calenviroscreen/california-releases-calenviroscreen-50-identify-and-prioritize-pollution-burdened-communities)). Fourth, **no tool tracks pending SF housing legislation alongside the code text in force**, as fast-moving SB 79 exemption updates show ([Soar](https://cpo.soar.com/articles/7875758/San-Francisco-County/California/Supervisors-approve-amendment-allowing-planning-director-to-update-SB-79-exemption-tables-pending-HCD-determinations)). Fifth, **no shared triage step tells a tenant which law covers their unit and which of the six or seven help providers to call** (an inference from the resident research, not from a survey) ([SF Rent Board](https://sf.gov/departments/rent-board); [HRCSF](https://www.hrcsf.org/know-your-rights)).

Positioning follows from the constraints. For **policy makers**, the free, approved incumbent is Copilot Chat, already cleared for Level 4 data ([sf.gov guidelines](https://www.sf.gov/san-francisco-generative-ai-guidelines)). A new tool wins only on what Copilot lacks: answers grounded in SF code sections, Legistar files, and DataSF dataset IDs, each linked and checkable. Because the guidelines ban relying on AI for legal review, the tool has to present itself as research support that a human verifies, which is the same workflow that made STARA acceptable ([Reboot Democracy](https://rebootdemocracy.ai/blog/sf-ai-regulatory-decluttering)). Working from public documents and open data avoids data-classification approvals. A city pilot would still need a 22J entry and would most likely enter through COIT's "Try Before You Buy" framework, if that framework launches *(unverified)* ([COIT](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/COIT_2026_ICT_Plan_Update_April_2026.pdf)). For **residents**, the tool must give legal information, not advice. It should prioritize procedural accuracy over disclaimers, and hand users off to the Rent Board, HRCSF, SFTU, or EDC rather than replace them. It also needs more than four non-English languages to beat the Rent Board's current reach. For **climate**, every flood answer must name its map and carry that map's limits: stormwater only for SFPUC, an SLR-plus-storm envelope with 2010 ground conditions for the 108" zone, and Bay side only for ART. A tool that quietly merges these layers would mislead.

The open questions that matter most for positioning are these: whether AmLegal or Copilot adds code-grounded search; whether the AI Workspace and AI Policy promised for late 2026 shipped; what changed in the guidelines revision dated September 30, 2026; whether housing departments use unreported AI; and what licenses apply to the ART and Urban Displacement data. All remain *(unverified)*.

## Conclusion

The gap is not a missing dataset or a missing chatbot. It is the missing **join**. Each layer, law, data, and climate, has a credible public steward, and none of them talks to the others. That changes the build question. Collecting data matters less, because DataSF and the state already publish it openly. What matters is grounding answers in the authoritative text and handling provenance: naming which code section, which dataset version, and which hazard map supports each sentence, and saying when a source is stale. That provenance is exactly what SF's guidelines, Stanford's quality rubric, and the RV-chatbot failure all demand.

STARA's 7-4 vote holds the less obvious lesson. In SF, AI-assisted legal analysis is technically accepted but politically exposed, and a supervisor who voted no still used a chatbot to read the bill. A tool that serves both sides, letting a skeptical supervisor and a worried tenant check the same cited sources, fits SF's governance more naturally than one that claims to decide anything. Policy makers and residents need the same evidence base with different interfaces, and that symmetry is the defensible position.
