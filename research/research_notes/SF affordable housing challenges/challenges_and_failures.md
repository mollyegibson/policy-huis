# San Francisco Affordable Housing: Challenges (as of Nov 2026) and Failed Proposals

Research date: 2026-11-04 (the day after the Nov 3, 2026 election). Figures from before 2024 are marked **[pre-2024]**. Several items come from advocacy groups (GrowSF, SPUR, 48hills, campaign-adjacent pieces) and are labeled as such.

**User-provided sources.** All four were fetched:
1. [ONE San Francisco Capital Plan FY2026-35, Affordable Housing](https://onesanfrancisco.org/cap-plan-2026/affordable-housing): bond history, agency roles, challenges. Gives no total investment figure.
2. [SF Planning, Housing Affordability Strategies (HAS)](https://sfplanning.org/housing-affordability-strategy): a 2018–2020 strategy **[pre-2024]**. Sets targets of 5,000 units/yr with at least 1/3 affordable, preservation of 1,100 units/yr, and $517M/yr of funding (2020 dollars).
3. [SPUR, "Realigning Housing Policy With Real-World Economics," Lori Droste, June 25, 2026](https://www.spur.org/news/2026-06-25/realigning-housing-policy-real-world-economics-san-francisco): the inclusionary reform package.
4. [Mission Local, Joe Rivano Barros, Nov 2, 2026 (updated Nov 3)](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/): affordability plans from officials and candidates.

## 1. Production vs. targets (RHNA, APR, Housing Inventory, Housing Element)

### Takeaway
San Francisco must zone for 82,069 units for 2023–2031, of which 46,598 are affordable. Through 2025 it had produced about 12% of that total, with roughly 37.5% of the cycle elapsed. Production fell to 1,457 units in 2024, the lowest since at least 2006. Very-low- and low-income progress stood at 14% each.

### Cited Findings
- **RHNA 2023–2031:** 82,069 units, or 94,300 with a recommended 15% buffer. Of these, 46,598 are to be affordable. Through 2025 the city had produced 12% of the target, while about 37.5% of the cycle had elapsed. Failing to show capacity "jeopardizes its local control over zoning as well as State funding." — [Budget & Legislative Analyst (BLA), "Funding and Incentivizing Affordable Housing in San Francisco," June 5, 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **SF's share of the region:** 82,069 of the Bay Area's 441,176 units. — [SF Planning RHNA Primer](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/citywide/housing-choice/RHNA-Primer.pdf)
- **Progress through 2025 by tier** (BLA Exhibit D, sourced to the [2025 SF Housing Inventory, published April 2026](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/resources/2026-04/2025_Housing_Inventory.pdf)). Source: [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)

  | Tier | Goal | Progress | Deficit | % |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Very low (<50% AMI) | 20,867 | 2,981 | 17,886 | 14% |
  | Low (50–80% AMI) | 12,014 | 1,682 | 10,332 | 14% |
  | Moderate (80–120% AMI) | 13,717 | 1,402 | 12,315 | 10% |
  | Above moderate | 35,471 | 3,516 | 31,955 | 10% |
  | **Total** | **82,069** | **9,581** | **72,488** | **12%** |

- **Total production:** It fell from a peak of 4,972 new units in 2017 to 1,457 in 2024. 2025 showed "a slight uptick" but remained well below pre-pandemic levels. — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf). SPUR reports that 2025 production was half of 2020 levels. — [SPUR June 25, 2026](https://www.spur.org/news/2026-06-25/realigning-housing-policy-real-world-economics-san-francisco)
- **New affordable units added per year (MOHCD portfolio):** 2,674 in 2018, 2,237 in 2019, 1,331 in 2020, 1,662 in 2021, 1,690 in 2022, 1,441 in 2023, 987 in 2024 and 1,218 in 2025. These values are read from BLA Exhibit C, so verify the mapping against the original chart. — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **Affordable stock:** MOHCD's portfolio held 36,301 units in 2026, about 8.6% of SF's 422,958 housing units. — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **Affordable pipeline:** MOHCD and OCII reported 10,148 affordable units in the pipeline as of February 2026. 60% of them (6,069 units) were still pre-construction. Planning counts 17,960 using a broader definition. — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **HCD's 2023 framing:** SF needs more than 10,000 homes a year, including more than 5,800 affordable. In 2023 it had permitted "less than one home per day." **[pre-2024, Oct 2023]** — [HCD press release](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/about-hcd/newsroom/state-releases-accountability-report-on-san-franciscos-housing-policies-and-practices)
- **Approved but unbuilt:** D8 candidate Darshini Patel says about 75,000 homes are approved, only 3,300 are under construction, and more than 20,000 are stalled in permitting. These are candidate claims and are unverified. — [Mission Local Nov 2, 2026](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **2022 Housing Element:** The Capital Plan says the Housing Element was updated in early 2023 **[pre-2024]**. — [Capital Plan FY2026-35](https://onesanfrancisco.org/cap-plan-2026/affordable-housing). The earlier HAS targets fed into it: 5,000 units/yr through 2050 with at least 1/3 affordable, 1,100 preserved/yr, and $517M/yr of funding. **[pre-2024]** — [SF Planning HAS](https://sfplanning.org/housing-affordability-strategy)
- **Prohousing designation:** SF received the state's Prohousing Designation on Oct 25, 2024, which gives it a scoring advantage for state funds. — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf), citing [sf.gov](https://www.sf.gov/news--san-francisco-receives-prohousing-designation-state-california)

### Inferences
- Meeting the affordable RHNA by 2031 would require about 6,756 affordable units a year (BLA Exhibit O). Recent output is about 1,000–1,700 a year, so the affordable gap is roughly 4–6x and is structural, not cyclical.
- The two pipeline counts (10,148 from MOHCD vs 17,960 from Planning) differ by definition. A cited-answer tool should name which one it uses.

### Gaps
- I did not open the official HCD Annual Progress Report (APR) for 2024 or 2025 directly. The tier figures above come from the BLA's citation of the 2025 Housing Inventory.
- Mission Local says the state goal is 82,069 units "by 2030", while the cycle runs to 2031. KQED gives 82,062 rather than 82,069. Both look like minor source inconsistencies.
- I did not extract the specific Housing Element program commitments (e.g., the number of rezoning programs).

## 2. Rezoning: Family Zoning Plan; HCD Policy & Practice Review

### Takeaway
The Board adopted the Family Zoning Plan 7–4 on Dec 2, 2025, finally adopted it Dec 9, and the mayor signed it Dec 12. That beat the state's Jan 31, 2026 deadline. The plan rezones about 96,000 parcels for about 36,000 units of capacity, mainly on the west and north sides. The city economist projects it will produce only about 14,600 units over 20 years. HCD's Oct 2023 review imposed 18 Required Actions. HCD said the city was up to date in Aug 2024. I found no 2026 status update.

### Cited Findings
- **Vote:** 7–4 on Dec 2, 2025. Yes: Mandelman, Mahmood, Melgar, Sauter, Dorsey, Sherrill, Wong. No: Fielder, Chen, Chan, Walton. — [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/news/12065708/san-francisco-supervisors-pass-rezoning-plan-making-way-for-taller-denser-housing)
- **Adoption timeline:** Final adoption Dec 9, 2025; signed Dec 12, 2025; state deadline Jan 31, 2026. — [GrowSF (advocacy)](https://growsf.org/news/2025-12-02-family-zoning-approved/); [SF Standard](https://sfstandard.com/2025/12/02/family-zoning-supervisors-vote-daniel-lurie/)
- **What it changes:**
  - About 96,000 parcels rezoned, with capacity for about 36,000 units focused on west and north side neighborhoods.
  - Two to four extra stories near transit and commercial corridors.
  - High-rises of 12 to 65 stories allowed on select corridors such as Van Ness, Market and Geary.
  - Melgar's amendment bars demolition of buildings with three or more rent-controlled units, shielding about 80,000 units. Chan's amendment to protect all rent-controlled units failed 7–4.
  - Without the plan, the state could withhold funding and override local decisions.

  — [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/news/12065708/san-francisco-supervisors-pass-rezoning-plan-making-way-for-taller-denser-housing)
- **Projected output:** The city's chief economist projects about 14,600 units over 20 years, roughly 732 a year. — [search summary of SF Standard/KQED coverage](https://sfstandard.com/2025/12/02/family-zoning-supervisors-vote-daniel-lurie/). D8 candidate Michael Nguyen and Darshini Patel repeat this estimate. — [Mission Local Nov 2, 2026](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **Critics' objections:**
  - The plan does little for affordable housing and could fuel speculation and displacement. Chen: it "does not solve the affordability crisis."
  - Small businesses get no aid if their leases are not renewed.

  — [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/news/12065708/san-francisco-supervisors-pass-rezoning-plan-making-way-for-taller-denser-housing)
- **Rent-controlled units at risk:** Chan argued that about 20,000 rent-controlled units remain susceptible to demolition. — [search summary of coverage](https://ktvu.com/news/luries-family-zoning-plan-approved-majority-san-francisco-supervisors). Candidate Patel calls for closing the "upzoning demolition loophole." — [Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **Background on passage:** see [SPUR, "How San Francisco's Family Zoning Plan Got Passed," Jan 28, 2026](https://www.spur.org/news/2026-01-28/how-san-franciscos-family-zoning-plan-got-passed) (not fetched) and [SF Planning project page](https://sfplanning.org/sf-family-zoning-plan).
- **HCD Policy & Practice Review, released Oct 25, 2023 [pre-2024]:**
  - Findings: SF has "the longest timelines and some of the highest procedural hurdles in California." Discretionary review causes delays, local politics harm outcomes, and some policies are inconsistent with state law.
  - It sets 18 Required Actions and 10 Recommended Actions in five categories: eliminate discretion and subjectivity; reform local CEQA practice; reform administrative appeals; standardize post-entitlement permitting; increase accountability and transparency.
  - The analysis was done by UC Berkeley IURD, led by Moira O'Neill.

  — [HCD press release](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/about-hcd/newsroom/state-releases-accountability-report-on-san-franciscos-housing-policies-and-practices); [full review PDF](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/policy-and-research/plan-report/sf-housing-policy-and-practice-review.pdf)
- **Compliance history:**
  - HCD issued a corrective-action letter after early deadlines were missed (Nov 28, 2023). — [HCD letter](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/planning-and-community/HAU/san-francisco-PPR-Corr-Action-112823.pdf)
  - HCD confirmed successful corrective action Jan 16, 2024. — [HCD letter](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/housing-for-all/housingelement-HCD-PPR-successful-corrective-action-011624.pdf)
  - In August 2024 HCD said SF was "up to date on required actions," and in some cases ahead of schedule. — [HCD newsroom](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/about-hcd/newsroom/housing-accountability-units-efforts-lead-to-san-franciscos-progress-removing-barriers-to-housing-production)
  - SF Planning's PPR tracker page was last updated Sep 11, 2025. — [sfplanning.org/ppr](https://sfplanning.org/ppr)

### Inferences
- There is a large gap between zoned capacity (about 36,000 units) and projected output (about 14,600 over 20 years). Rezoning removes a legal constraint but not the financial one (see section 3). This matches HCD's 2023 point that delays combine with costs.

### Gaps
- I found no 2026 status update on the remaining PPR Required Actions.
- I did not verify how the Family Zoning Plan's local density program interacts with the state density bonus.

## 3. Financing and cost

### Takeaway
A tax-credit-financed affordable unit cost about $954,000 on average in 2024. The city's share is roughly $300,000 per unit, about 32%. The BLA estimates $17.9B in city subsidy is needed to meet the remaining affordable RHNA by 2031, and found no source that could supply it. Local funding relies on voter-approved bonds and one-time money. The $20B regional bond was pulled in Aug 2024. Nov 2026 Props C and I sought permanent local streams; their outcomes were not yet reported when this was researched.

### Cited Findings
- **Per-unit cost and funding mix:** The average LIHTC affordable project cost $954,127 per unit in 2024. Funding shares:

  | Source | Per unit | Share |
  |---|---|---|
  | Federal (incl. LIHTC) | $443,108 | 46% |
  | City | $300,027 | 32% |
  | State | $118,390 | 13% |
  | Permanent loans | $87,431 | 8% |

  — [BLA June 2026, Exhibit F](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **Funding gap:** Meeting the remaining affordable RHNA (40,533 units) by 2031 needs $17.9B in city subsidy, about 6,756 units a year. This assumes $900,000 per unit and city subsidies of $405K–$490K. The BLA found no source or strategy that "would produce $17.9 billion" by 2031. — [BLA June 2026, Exhibit O](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **Older baseline:** The 2012 city economist estimated about $250,000 per subsidized unit. **[pre-2024]** — [Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **Stalled pipeline:** D8 candidate Nguyen claims 10,100 affordable homes in 190 pipeline projects are stalled by a $25.1B gap. This is a candidate claim, unverified. — [Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **FY2024-25 MOHCD spending:** $285.7M on multifamily grants and loans, of which about $234M came from six local sources. G.O. bonds and debt are the largest. Bonds are "not a secure funding source": they compete with other city debt and need voter approval each time. — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **Bond and funding history:**
  - Affordable housing G.O. bonds: $310M in 2015, $600M in 2019 (Prop A), $300M in 2024 (Prop A).
  - The 2020 Health and Recovery bond included $147M for permanent supportive housing.
  - The 2022 Certificates of Participation included $112M for affordable housing.
  - The 2018 gross receipts tax funds homelessness housing and services (Prop C 2018, "Our City Our Home").

  — [Capital Plan FY2026-35](https://onesanfrancisco.org/cap-plan-2026/affordable-housing)
- **Prop A (March 5, 2024):** Passed with about 70%. $300M in bonds for about 1,400 affordable units. The same ballot's Prop C, a transfer-tax exemption for commercial-to-residential conversions, passed with about 53%. — [Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/san-francisco/affordable-housing/san-francisco-voters-pass-affordable-housing-bonds-conversion-tax-exemption-123275)
- **Prop C 2018 / OCOH:** At least 50% of the Our City Our Home Fund is mandated for permanent supportive housing. — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **Prop I (2020) transfer tax** **[pre-2024 origin]:**
  - It raised the tax on sales over $10M from 2.75% to 5.5%, and on sales of $25M or more from 3% to 6%.
  - It is a general tax, so there is no legal mandate to spend it on housing.
  - Prop K (2020) authorized up to 10,000 units of city-owned social housing. A Nov 2020 Board resolution called for Prop I revenue to go to social housing.
  - Projected revenue is more than $110M a year.

  — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf); [Local News Matters, Sep 25, 2026](https://localnewsmatters.org/2026/09/25/san-francisco-prop-i-affordable-housing/)
- **Housing Trust Fund:** The city contributes about $50.8–52M a year. — [GrowSF Prop C guide (advocacy)](https://growsf.org/voter-guide/san-francisco-voter-guide-november-2026-election/measures/prop-c/); [BLA](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **Nov 3, 2026 Prop C (Housing Trust Fund charter amendment):**
  - Contribution rises from FY2028-29 toward $125M a year, then grows by at most 3% a year.
  - The Board can freeze contributions when the projected deficit exceeds $250M, or cut them by 10% in years the Rainy Day Reserve is used.
  - It extends the sunset from 2043 to 2058 and raises the down-payment-loan income cap from 120% to 200% AMI.
  - Controller: about $6M in added cost in FY2028-29, rising to about $60M a year by FY2034-35. It does not comply with the city's non-binding policy against spending set-asides.
  - The Board placed it on the ballot 11–0 on July 14, 2026 (File 260537). It needs a simple majority.

  — [GrowSF voter guide (advocacy)](https://growsf.org/voter-guide/san-francisco-voter-guide-november-2026-election/measures/prop-c/)
- **Nov 3, 2026 Prop I ("Affordable Housing Guarantee Act"):**
  - It dedicates 50% of transfer-tax revenue on sales over $10M to affordable and social housing and rental assistance. Split: at least 60% production, at least 25% acquisition/rehab, at least 10% tenant stabilization.
  - The Controller estimates about $120M a year diverted from the General Fund.
  - It would remove the Board's power to amend the transfer tax without voters.
  - Led by DSA SF; backed by Fielder, Walton, SEIU 1021 and the SF Tenants Union. Opposed by Mayor Lurie and the police and fire unions.

  — [Local News Matters, Sep 25, 2026](https://localnewsmatters.org/2026/09/25/san-francisco-prop-i-affordable-housing/)
- **Ballot-wording dispute:** City Hall and some unions fought over describing the measure as "social housing" vs. "affordable housing." — [Mission Local, Aug 2026](https://missionlocal.org/2026/08/mayors-office-unions-affordable-housing-wording/) (not fetched; title only)
- **BUILD Act:** Lurie and Mahmood proposed it in Feb 2026 to roll the transfer tax back to pre-2020 rates. It was shelved in June 2026 over budget concerns. — [Local News Matters](https://localnewsmatters.org/2026/09/25/san-francisco-prop-i-affordable-housing/)
- **State and federal tax credits:**
  - In 2025 CTCAC allocated $31.6M in state LIHTC to two SF projects (280 units).
  - The 2025 federal "One Big Beautiful Bill" lowered the 4% LIHTC bond test from 50% to 25% of aggregate basis, "functionally doubling" 4% LIHTC availability.
  - State HCD/CalHFA programs (MHP, AHSC, IIG, NPLH) have provided more than $1.2B to SF since 2018.

  — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **Federal credit expansion:** Rep. Mullin says the Affordable Housing Credit Improvement Act "was recently enacted," supporting about 1.6M homes nationally over 10 years. This is the congressman's claim. — [Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **City-controllable costs:** Sup. Sherrill says transfer taxes and permitting delays make SF projects about 25% more expensive than in other major cities. This is an official's claim. — [Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **Financing options the BLA identified:**
  1. Revolving loan fund, modeled on Montgomery County, MD
  2. Joint Powers Authority combining MOHCD and the Housing Authority
  3. Open indenture
  4. Municipal bank
  5. More Enhanced Infrastructure Financing Districts (EIFDs)
  6. SB 593 bonding
  7. Directing Prop I revenue to social housing
  8. Raising the debt ceiling
  9. Enhancing the Housing Trust Fund
  10. New taxes

  — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)

### Inferences
- Even if both Nov 2026 measures passed, together they would add roughly $75M (Prop C, by FY2034-35) plus about $120M a year (Prop I). That is roughly $200M a year against an annual need of about $3B (BLA: $1.2B + $0.77B + $1.0B). So money remains the binding constraint.

### Gaps
- **Nov 3, 2026 results for Props C and I (and Prop B, public bank) were not yet indexed** when this was researched (Nov 4). Check [sfelections.gov/results](https://www.sf.gov/november-3-2026-consolidated-general-election). Certification is due Dec 3, 2026.
- I found no SF-specific data on CDLAC tax-exempt bond competition (oversubscription, SF projects rejected) for 2025–26.
- I found no dollar amounts for the Prop C 2018 (OCOH) allocations or for the 2019 Prop A spend-down status.

## 4. Inclusionary requirements and fee changes

### Takeaway
The Controller's 2026 triennial study found almost no new housing financially feasible and set the maximum supportable inclusionary rate at 0%. The city then cut the on-site requirement to 5%, tied politically to the Prop C Trust Fund increase. That change is in effect from Aug 22, 2026. Inclusionary fee revenue went from more than $28M a year before the pandemic to slightly negative in FY2024-25.

### Cited Findings
- **2026 Controller memo:**
  - Transmitted Apr 30, 2026. The TAC recommended 5% on-site if a long-term voter-approved funding measure passes.
  - Requirements "significantly above 0%" would threaten feasibility and would not create more affordable housing.

  — [Controller Triennial Economic Feasibility Report 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/Triennial_Economic_Feasibilty_Report_2026.final.pdf) (via search summary; full PDF not read); [SPUR June 1, 2026](https://www.spur.org/news/2026-06-01/report-highlights-math-keeps-housing-being-built-san-francisco)
- **BLA on the TAC:** The 2026 TAC concluded "nearly all new residential projects are infeasible" and the "maximum recommended inclusionary requirement is zero percent." — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **TAC package (unanimous):**
  1. 5% on-site cap, or 10% if no affordable measure is on the Nov 2026 ballot
  2. Exempt projects under 25 units
  3. Drop the middle-income tier
  4. Treat rentals and condos alike
  5. Set the in-lieu fee above the on-site rate
  6. Waive fees on state density bonus units
  7. Cut impact fees 67%

  Sponsored by Lurie and Supervisors Melgar, Dorsey, Sherrill and Sauter. — [SPUR June 25, 2026](https://www.spur.org/news/2026-06-25/realigning-housing-policy-real-world-economics-san-francisco)
- **Enactment:**
  - The Board cut the rate from 15% to 5% on July 14, 2026. This was the first half of a May 2026 Melgar/Lurie deal in which affordable-housing nonprofits dropped their opposition in exchange for the Trust Fund increase. — [GrowSF (advocacy)](https://growsf.org/voter-guide/san-francisco-voter-guide-november-2026-election/measures/prop-c/)
  - Planning says it took effect Aug 22, 2026 (File 260538, Ord. 136-26). — [SF Planning Inclusionary program page](https://sfplanning.org/inclusionary-affordable-housing-program) (via search summary)
  - Context: [The VoiceSF on the "grand bargain"](https://thevoicesf.org/melgar-lurie-push-restart-button-on-affordable-housing-grand-bargain-with-new-ballot-measure/) (not fetched)
- **2023 temporary reductions:**
  - The Board approved the Temporary Fee Reduction Program on Sep 5, 2023.
  - On-site rates for 25+ unit rentals went from 22% to 12% for pipeline projects.
  - Impact fees were cut 33% (Ord. 187-23). 80–85% of fees were deferred to first certificate of occupancy (Ord. 193-23). Fee indexing changed to a flat 2%.

  **[pre-2024 origin]** — [BLA June 2026, Exhibits J–K](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **Effect of the 2023 changes:** The discounts saved only 0.44–1.83% of total development cost, up to about $16,433 per unit. "Fee discounts did not make projects feasible at scale." The decline in revenue is driven more by market conditions than by the fee cuts. — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **In-lieu fee revenue by fiscal year:** FY2018-19 $31.4M, FY2019-20 $28.9M, FY2020-21 $4.9M, FY2023-24 $1.5M, FY2024-25 –$1.27M (refunds). — [BLA June 2026, Exhibit I](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf). SPUR gives similar figures: more than $20M a year pre-pandemic, just over $2M a year since 2020, and negative recently. — [SPUR June 25, 2026](https://www.spur.org/news/2026-06-25/realigning-housing-policy-real-world-economics-san-francisco)
- **Market conditions:**
  - Average 1BR rent fell from $3,212 (2019) to $2,595 (2021), then rose to $3,164 (Jan 2026).
  - As of May 2026, outlets reported a surge in rent attributed to AI firms.

  — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
  - Mission Local reports rents up 24% year over year and a median 1BR of $3,879 (Apartment List). — [Mission Local Nov 2026](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **BLA recommendations:**
  - Broaden the TAC's remit beyond inclusionary rates.
  - Use time-limited moratoria that can be reinstated, rather than permanent cuts.
  - Improve fee tracking.

  — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rising AI-driven rents in 2026 could restore feasibility. That would revive the debate over whether the 5% rate is too low. The BLA's "reinstate when conditions improve" recommendation anticipates this.
- **Conflict to flag:** GrowSF describes the July 14 change as "15% to 5%," while the BLA's table shows pre-2026 rates of 12–18% depending on project size and timing. "15%" is a simplification.

### Gaps
- I did not read the Controller's full 2026 report, so I do not have its residual land value figures.
- I did not confirm the exact final ordinance provisions (e.g., whether the under-25-unit exemption and the 67% fee cut were enacted as recommended).

## 5. State laws in play

### Takeaway
State streamlining (SB 35/SB 423, AB 2011, density bonus, builder's remedy, AB 1633) and the June 2025 CEQA overhaul (AB 130/SB 131) shrink SF's discretionary veto. SF-specific labor rules still apply. I found no SF data on how much these tools are used.

### Cited Findings
- **AB 130 and SB 131 (signed June 30, 2025, effective immediately):**
  - AB 130 creates a statutory CEQA exemption for qualifying infill housing on sites up to 20 acres (PRC §21080.66).
  - SB 131 streamlines review for "near-miss" projects that fail one condition.
  - In SF, projects of 50 or more units up to 85 ft are subject to AB 2011 labor standards.

  — [Holland & Knight, July 2, 2025](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/07/california-legislature-enacts-major-ceqa-reforms-for-housing-rich); [Nixon Peabody, June 5, 2026](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2026/06/05/california-ab-130-shapes-ceqa-exemptions-pathways-to-infill-housing) (via search summaries)
- **AB 1633:** It lets developers sue cities that block infill housing by demanding further CEQA review. YIMBY Law's Sonja Trauss called it "a direct result" of the 469 Stevenson dispute. — [SF Standard, Apr 9, 2024](https://sfstandard.com/2024/04/09/yimby-nimby-nordstrom-469-stevenson-settlement/)
- **Wiener's claim:** Sen. Wiener says his laws have cut average SF approval times by 80%. This is his own claim, unverified. — [Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **Local CEQA appeals:** Sup. Mahmood's SHADE Act would streamline local CEQA appeals. He says these appeals have blocked or delayed more than 2,000 units (his claim). — [Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **Builder's remedy precedent:** The 2022 court ruling in the 469 Stevenson case held that state housing laws do not apply until environmental review is certified. **[pre-2024]** — [Planetizen, Nov 2022](https://www.planetizen.com/news/2022/11/119527-court-ruling-setback-californias-builders-remedy)

### Gaps
- I found no SF-specific counts of projects filed under SB 423, AB 2011, builder's remedy or AB 130.
- I did not verify the state density bonus details.

## 6. Tenant-side challenges

### Takeaway
37% of SF renter households were rent-burdened in 2024, with 18% severely burdened. Rents rose about 24% year over year in 2026. Officials report rising evictions, mostly for nonpayment. Policy responses center on right to counsel, small-debt eviction limits, banked-rent caps, and DAHLIA lottery access.

### Cited Findings
- **Rent burden (2024):** 37% of renter households were rent-burdened: 19% paid over 30% of income and 18% paid over 50%. — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **Rents and evictions:** Rents are up 24% year over year (Apartment List) and the median 1BR is $3,879. Evictions are rising. Sup. Fielder says evictions are at a 10-year high, mostly for nonpayment. — [Mission Local Nov 2026](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **Tenant legislation cited by officials:**
  - Fielder: bar evictions for debts under one month's Fair Market Rent.
  - Lurie and Sauter: cap banked rent increases.
  - Mahmood: No Hidden Rent Act.
  - Lurie and City Attorney Chiu: expand Tenant Right to Counsel.
  - Melgar, Mandelman and Nguyen: fund right to counsel fully.

  — [Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **Right to counsel:** It kept 93% of clients housed and 1,539 households (July 2023–June 2024) from homelessness. — [Bay Legal, June 2, 2025](https://baylegal.org/tenant-right-to-counsel-prevents-homelessness/). Flat funding supports only about 2,500 cases, while legal aid faces cuts as evictions rise. — [SF Public Press](https://www.sfpublicpress.org/eviction-rates-in-sf-soar-as-legal-aid-faces-deep-funding-cuts) (via search summary; date unverified)
- **DAHLIA lottery:** Completed projects lease through the DAHLIA lottery or by direct referral from HSH/SFHA. Priorities include households displaced by fire or no-fault eviction. Many tenants fear asking for unit modifications because of eviction risk. — [BLA June 2026](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf)
- **Equity:** BIPOC residents face higher rates of homelessness, rent burden, substandard housing and overcrowding, linked to urban renewal and redlining. — [Capital Plan FY2026-35](https://onesanfrancisco.org/cap-plan-2026/affordable-housing). The number of low- and moderate-income households has declined. **[pre-2024]** — [SF Planning HAS](https://sfplanning.org/housing-affordability-strategy)

### Gaps
- I have not verified Rent Board eviction-notice counts for 2025–26; the "10-year high" is Fielder's claim.
- I have no data on DAHLIA application-to-unit ratios, habitability complaints, or the FY2026-27 right-to-counsel budget.

## 7. Failed proposals and why

### Takeaway
SF's notable failures share three causes:
- Board discretion over individual projects (469 Stevenson, 450 O'Farrell)
- Rival ballot measures splitting the vote (Props D and E, 2022)
- Legal and threshold risk on regional bonds (RM4, 2024)

In 2025–26 the main casualties were the BUILD Act (shelved) and the prior inclusionary levels. Some appeals against 100% affordable projects were also rejected, which let those projects proceed.

### Cited Findings
- **469 Stevenson (Oct 2021) [pre-2024]:**
  - The Board voted 8–3 to overturn the Planning Commission's CEQA approval of a 27-story, 495-unit project (89 affordable) on a Nordstrom parking lot. — [SFist, Oct 27, 2021](https://sfist.com/2021/10/27/supes-shoot-down-27-story-soma-residential-tower-over-earthquake-displacement-concerns); [The Real Deal](https://therealdeal.com/sanfrancisco/2021/10/28/sf-supervisors-reject-builds-monster-on-sixth-street-drawing-mayors-ire)
  - Stated reasons: seismic and geotechnical concerns, and gentrification/displacement. TODCO (John Elberling) was the appellant.
  - Aftermath: The court rejected YIMBY Law's suit in Oct 2022. — [SFist](https://sfist.com/2022/10/31/judge-pretty-much-shoots-down-yimby-lawsuit-against-sf-over-rejected-high-rise-at-nordstrom-parking-lot). Planning re-approved the project after about 18 more months of review. YIMBY Law settled and paid the city $32,000 (reported Apr 9, 2024). The site remains a vacant lot, and TODCO cites the difficulty of financing high-rises. AB 1633 followed. — [SF Standard, Apr 9, 2024](https://sfstandard.com/2024/04/09/yimby-nimby-nordstrom-469-stevenson-settlement/)
- **450–474 O'Farrell (late 2021) [pre-2024]:** Also blocked. Combined with 469 Stevenson, more than 800 units (about 130 affordable) were lost. HCD warned the city. — [SF Standard, Jan 10, 2022](https://sfstandard.com/2022/01/10/san-francisco-scrambles-to-prep-for-lawsuits-after-rejecting-800-new-housing-units/); [HCD letter Nov 22, 2021](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/community-development/housing-element/docs/sfrsanfrancisco-loi-ta-112221.pdf)
- **Props D and E (Nov 2022) [pre-2024]:**
  - Both were streamlining measures. D (Breed's) required less of developers. E (Board progressives') paired streamlining with higher affordability.
  - With about 95% counted, D had 48.8% and E 45.5%. Both needed a majority, and both failed. — [Local News Matters, Nov 9, 2022](https://localnewsmatters.org/2022/11/09/dueling-san-francisco-housing-propositions-remain-in-the-balance-both-shy-of-50-percent/); [SFist, Nov 14, 2022](https://sfist.com/2022/11/14/final-local-ballot-measures-called-prop-m-vacancy-tax-wins-prop-e-affordable-housing-measure-falls)
  - Explanations: GrowSF says E was placed to confuse voters and sink D. Wiener called E a "do-nothing sabotage measure." D had about $2.6M and still lost. These are partisan framings; I found no neutral post-mortem. — [GrowSF report](https://report.growsf.org/p/election-results); [SF Standard](https://sfstandard.com/2022/11/08/props-d-e-election-night/)
- **Bay Area $20B bond (Regional Measure 4, BAHFA), withdrawn mid-Aug 2024:**
  - Reasons given:
    - A taxpayer lawsuit that forced the annual cost estimate up from about $670M to $911M
    - Its dependence on statewide Prop 5, which would have lowered the vote threshold to 55%
    - Polling around 55% against a two-thirds requirement
  - Supporters retargeted 2026.

  — [SF Standard, Aug 14, 2024](https://sfstandard.com/2024/08/14/bahfa-bond-yanked-at-last-minute/); [CalMatters, Aug 2024](https://calmatters.org/housing/2024/08/bay-area-housing-bond-pulled/); [Daily Journal](https://dailyjournal.com/articles/380392-bay-area-housing-authority-pulls-ballot-measure-after-taxpayer-lawsuit); [CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/regional-measure-4-bay-area-20b-housing-bond-pulled-november-24-ballot/)
  - Sources differ in emphasis: the legal problems vs. fear of losing at the polls.
  - D10 candidate Brookter backs "future regional BAHFA funding." — [Mission Local](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/san-francisco-housing-affordability-plans/)
- **BUILD Act (2026):** Proposed by Lurie and Mahmood in Feb 2026 to roll back the 2020 Prop I transfer-tax rates. Shelved in June 2026 amid budget concerns. — [Local News Matters, Sep 25, 2026](https://localnewsmatters.org/2026/09/25/san-francisco-prop-i-affordable-housing/)
- **Chan's rent-control amendment (Dec 2025):** It would have protected all rent-controlled units in the Family Zoning Plan from demolition. It failed 7–4. — [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/news/12065708/san-francisco-supervisors-pass-rezoning-plan-making-way-for-taller-denser-housing)
- **First inclusionary "grand bargain" attempt:** The Board vote was preceded by an earlier failed deal; the VoiceSF headline describes a "restart." — [The VoiceSF](https://thevoicesf.org/melgar-lurie-push-restart-button-on-affordable-housing-grand-bargain-with-new-ballot-measure/) (not fetched; details unverified)
- **Appeals rejected, so projects proceeded:**
  - July 2025: the Board unanimously rejected an appeal against the 100%-affordable "Marvel in the Mission" (16th & Mission). — [Mission Local, July 2025](https://missionlocal.org/2025/07/supervisors-reject-neighbors-attempt-to-challenge-permanent-supportive-housing-at-mission-and-16th/)
  - Feb 2026: an appeal of a 70-unit affordable senior project at 3333 Mission was unanimously rejected. (via search summary; source URL not confirmed)
- **Earlier failed measures:** The 2015 Prop I Mission moratorium on market-rate housing was defeated **[pre-2024]**. — [KTVU](https://www.ktvu.com/news/san-francisco-re-elects-mayor-rejects-props-f-and-i)

### Inferences
- Since 2023, SF's failures have shifted. Discretionary vetoes are now constrained by state law (AB 1633, the PPR, AB 130). What fails now are projects that can't be financed (469 Stevenson re-approved but unbuilt) and measures that fall short of voter thresholds.

### Gaps
- I found no 2024–2026 instance of the Board or Planning Commission denying a housing project outright.
- I could not confirm any June 2026 SF housing ballot measure that failed. The June 2, 2026 election rejected two tax measures about 55–45 that do not appear to be housing measures. — [El Reporter, June 5, 2026](https://elreporterosf.com/san-francisco-approves-infrastructure-bond-term-limits-rejects-tax-measures/)
- The Nov 3, 2026 outcomes (Props B, C and I) were unknown when this was researched.
- I could not fetch the SF Examiner's final 2022 D/E article (HTTP 429), so certified final percentages are not confirmed.

## 8. Questions policy makers and residents publicly ask (use cases for a cited-answer tool)

### Takeaway
Recurring public questions, drawn from Mission Local's Nov 2026 survey, Board debates and analyst reports, are mostly factual and checkable. Partisan claims about these questions often conflict, which makes them suited to a cited-answer tool.

- **"How many homes will rezoning actually produce?"** The capacity is 36,000 and the economist's projection is 14,600 over 20 years. - **"Did cutting inclusionary rates and fees produce housing, or just lose affordable units and revenue?"** — [BLA](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BLA_Financing_Affordable_Housing_060526.pdf); [SPUR](https://www.spur.org/news/2026-06-25/realigning-housing-policy-real-world-economics-san-francisco)

### Inferences
- The highest-value features for a cited-answer tool:
  - reconciling conflicting official numbers (pipeline counts, unit projections, RHNA totals of 82,062 vs 82,069)
  - separating claims (candidate, advocacy) from sourced figures
  - tracking measure and ordinance status over time (PPR actions, Ord. 136-26, Props C and I)

### Gaps
